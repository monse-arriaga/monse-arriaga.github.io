Research

I’m interested in building projects that don’t only answer a scientific question but also, in some way, tell a story. World model benchmarks generate thousands of videos that are expensive to make, often evaluated by hand, and that nobody really uses afterwards. I would like the datasets and the information I generate to create a story and that outlives the question that produced it.

Right now the question I care about is time: change over time and its periodicity. How time works inside world models, which structures help them count and keep a period, and how time-related hallucinations live inside the model’s virtual world.

Western Bluebird detection in the wild

Can a detector find a small bird in a 4K frame? Ecologists filming nest boxes need to know when birds arrive, fight and leave, and annotating that video by hand is slow. I built a benchmark from fixed-camera footage of Western Bluebirds (Sialia mexicana) recorded during a nest-competition experiment in La Malinche National Park, Tlaxcala: 6,016 annotated frames with 7,637 birds, most of them under 0.3% of the image.

I compared supervised detectors (YOLOv8, YOLO26, Faster R-CNN, RT-DETR), open-vocabulary models used zero-shot and fine-tuned (OWL-ViT, YOLO-World, Grounding DINO) and segmentation models (Mask R-CNN, YOLOv8-Seg, Grounded-SAM, SAM 3).

  • Supervised detectors are the most reliable. Faster R-CNN has the best mAP@0.5:0.95 (0.47).
  • Open-vocabulary models almost fail zero-shot, but fine-tuned YOLO-World reaches the best mAP@0.5 (0.94).
  • Failures depend on brightness, contrast, crowding and blur together, not only on object size.

My role: I designed the benchmark, built the training and evaluation pipeline, ran the experiments and annotated data.

Worlds in Slow Motion

Do video world models respond to a physical event, or do they just keep the scene moving? We filmed real, calibrated experiments (falls, collisions, ramps, pendulums), showed the models the moment right before the event, and measured what they generated against what actually happened. First results with four models. The next round needs more prompts, more seeds and more human annotation, which means more compute and more people.

Work in progress. The preprint on arXiv is an early version with fewer experiments, and its results are not final. I’m expanding the dataset and revising the analysis, so numbers and conclusions may change.

My role: I designed the benchmark, built the measurement pipeline, ran the experiments and annotated generations.

The memory of political discourse

How does political discourse on X/Twitter organize itself, and how does it change from one year to the next? I built a corpus of 234,134 tweets from 19 Mexican political and media accounts (2018–2024), embedded them with BGE-M3, and found 214 semantic clusters with UMAP and HDBSCAN. Then I used modern Hopfield networks as a memory for each political year and asked which memory a new post “belongs” to.

  • One average per year is not enough. Memories built from topic × month keep much more of the structure of each year.
  • A hybrid memory (Hopfield memories for clustered posts and a linear classifier for scattered ones) gave the best balance of compression, interpretability and temporal recall.
  • Posts that clustering calls “noise” still carry temporal signal: they are scattered discourse, not empty content.
  • The temporal signal comes from what is said, not only from who is saying it.

Not published as a paper because of the nature of the data (tweets). Only aggregated results are shown.

My role: Sole author: corpus, pipeline, experiments and analysis.
