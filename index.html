Hi 👋
Intro taco$ 1 min

I'm Monse Arriaga, and I study how intelligent systems learn, reason, and see the world. I'm a Computer Science student at the National Autonomous University of Mexico (UNAM), fascinated by how machines learn to perceive and make sense of the world. My work moves between academia and industry: research collaborations on one side, practical technology projects on the other, and I'm happiest working somewhere in that overlap.

Taco of things I like$ 2 min

I'm especially drawn to computer vision, multimodal AI, world models, and representation learning, along with the trickier question of whether we can really trust and interpret what these systems do. I also study English Literature, which keeps me thinking about language and meaning from a completely different direction.

What I'm cooking$ time

I’m interested in building projects that don’t only answer a scientific question but also, in some way, tell a story. World model benchmarks generate thousands of videos that are expensive to make, often evaluated by hand, and that nobody really uses afterwards. I would like the datasets and the information I generate to create a story and that outlives the question that produced it.

Right now the question I care about is time: change over time and its periodicity. How time works inside world models, which structures help them count and keep a period, and how time-related hallucinations live inside the model’s virtual world.

After-work taco$ 1 book

Outside of research I like playing video games, mostly LEGO Batman, Disco Elysium and Baldur's Gate. When there's nothing to do I read, and I like taking care of my chinchilla.

Worlds in Slow Motion

in progress

Do video world models respond to a physical event, or do they just keep the scene moving? We filmed real, calibrated experiments (falls, collisions, ramps, pendulums), showed the models the moment right before the event, and measured what they generated against what actually happened. First results with four models. The next round needs more prompts, more seeds and more human annotation, which means more compute and more people.

Can a video model keep time when you ask it to? A benchmark that measures it frame by frame across several current image-to-video models, with a human check on the automatic measurement. Paper in preparation, not on arXiv yet.
